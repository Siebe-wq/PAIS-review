This is the standard every review on this site is written against. It is deliberately
specific to infection-associated chronic illnesses, because the usual generic appraisal
checklists miss most of what goes wrong in this field.

## Sample selection

### ME/CFS

Inclusion criteria:

- Must include PEM, ideally operationalised with a validated questionnaire (DSQ-2 is best).
- ICC, CCC, IOM strongly preferred. The paper must state how this was operationalised or ascertained.
- Fukuda/CDC'94 is unacceptable, because it does not have PEM as necessary. It can inflate
  the sample by 1.7x (Nacul, 2011), or much more if operationalised with low thresholds for
  severity and frequency.

### Long Covid

- At least 3 months, ideally more than 6 months.
- Clarify hospitalisation status. Non-hospitalised cohorts are the most interesting.
- Must say how COVID infection was confirmed: PCR > RAT > exposure to a confirmed case plus
  symptoms > physician diagnosis > self-report.

### All studies

All studies must note status on ME/CFS, POTS/OI and MCAS.

Also note:

- Symptom severity.
- Duration of illness, which may affect recovery rates and even biomarkers.
- Whether the study population is severe enough and representative of the patient population.
- Ideally severity is quantified with a scale such as FUNCAP or Bell. Not all of these scales
  are equally good.

Mental health screening: the questionnaire should be appropriate for a disabled population. A
questionnaire that uses "sometimes I can't get out of bed" or "I don't have much hope for the
future" to diagnose depression is not appropriate. PHQ-2 is supposedly good.

Controls: sedentary or inactive is good, ideally even deconditioned. It is often helpful to
also have controls with a different disease, to decide whether findings are disease-specific.

## Study design

### Outcome measures

An important point: patient-reported outcomes are not the only ones susceptible to bias. Many
supposedly objective measures are susceptible to *performance bias*, where people try harder
because they expect they can do more, expect less PEM, or want to please.

High risk of performance bias:

- 6-minute walk test
- Grip strength
- Any other "simple" exercise performance
- Real-time brain activity

Lower but still some risk of effort or subjective effects:

- Daily step count
- Work hours
- Sleep data
- Daily time upright
- Arm movement
- Probably some exercise metrics, such as the difference in VO2max on a 2-day CPET

Actually objective:

- Blood levels
- Imaging
- Cerebral blood flow
- Lean test
- More sophisticated exercise performance analysis, such as oxygen extraction and other things
  clearly out of the participant's control

Objective does not mean free from bias. A lean test does not take into account the exertion of
getting to the testing site, so the delta HR is often lower because the resting HR is already
somewhat elevated. Blood levels vary across the day. Images need to be interpreted.

Fatigue scales are not always very valid. The same goes for depression scales that include
items like "I have trouble getting out of bed", or anxiety scales that include "I worry about
the future".

For cerebral blood flow: was velocity measured, or volume?

### Trials

- Was it pre-registered?
- Has the protocol been adhered to? Has the protocol been changed?

### Data collection

Measuring PEM status at each data collection point is essential. In ME/CFS, the state of the
patient (PEM, no PEM, a little PEM) probably affects disease markers.

### Histopathology

Was the morphological scoring done with a predefined, validated classification scheme? Was
there inter-rater reliability testing? Was there any automated morphological analysis?

## Bias

The biggest source of bias is the preconceptions and wishes of the study authors. It is also a
risk factor for fraud. So: is there a bias, and how big is it?

Disappointingly, this is not included in standard risk-of-bias analyses.

## Plausibility

Are the findings consistent with the results of other studies? Only take into account those
that are methodologically sound. What would these results predict other methods to pick up?
Generate hypotheses.

## Theory

Any hypothesis of ME/CFS should include a thorough discussion of four questions:

1. How does the explanation fit with the **multimodality** of PEM triggers? That is, how does
   it explain that the clinical picture of PEM can be induced by physical, cognitive, mental,
   orthostatic, emotional, chronobiological and sensory stressors alike?
2. How does the hypothesis explain that such **disparate triggers** lead to the **same**
   clinical picture? Specifically, can it explain that muscle fatigue is part of PEM even when
   the muscles are not used at all?
3. How does it explain the **specific delay in onset** that defines PEM?
4. How does it explain the **typical duration** of PEM?

Use the "natural experiment" test systematically. For any proposed mechanism, ask: is there a
condition where this mechanism is known to be disrupted in isolation? What does that condition
look like? Does it produce PEM?

Use the "drug experiment" test systematically. For any proposed mechanism, ask: is there a drug
that would fix this mechanism? If that drug has been tried in ME/CFS by a reasonable number of
patients, did it produce the results the hypothesis predicts?

## Miscellaneous

### In vitro and animal efficacy papers

- Calculate whether the concentrations are clinically realistic. Can they be reached? Will
  there be toxicity issues, and how close to CC50? Are the pharmacokinetics reasonable? You
  cannot continuously inhibit a function when the half-life is very short.
- For animal models, note the mediocre predictive capacity of animal research.

### Circular reasoning

Watch for it. For example, using test results to make diagnoses and then using those diagnoses
to validate the tests.

### Epidemiology

Often skewed by:

- Response bias
- A broad case definition, such as Long Covid meaning any symptom of any severity after an
  unconfirmed infection at more than 4 weeks
- Not accounting for recovery rate
- Simplistically multiplying an unrealistically high incidence rate by confirmed cases, which
  are an underestimate, to get a total case count

Also ask: does it have appropriate controls? Is it a prospective design?

### Other disease-specific considerations

Could hypovolemia affect the comparability of blood levels?
