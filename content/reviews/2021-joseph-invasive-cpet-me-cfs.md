## Study design

This is a retrospective analysis of clinical records. The authors went through 1,516 invasive cardiopulmonary exercise tests (iCPET) done between 2011 and 2018 at the Brigham and Women's Hospital Dyspnea Clinic in Boston. In an iCPET, catheters are placed in the pulmonary artery and the radial artery. The patient then cycles upright to exhaustion while breathing gases and blood pressures are measured, and blood is sampled once a minute. This lets the test measure cardiac output (Qc, the amount of blood the heart pumps per minute), filling pressures in the heart, and how much oxygen the body takes out of the blood. Very few studies in ME/CFS have used this method.

The authors also used skin biopsies from the lower leg, taken when the referring physician chose to order one. These were stained for nerve fibres (with an antibody against PGP9.5) to test for small fiber neuropathy (SFN), damage to the thin nerves that sense pain and temperature and help control blood vessels.

The analysis had three parts:

1. Patients with ME/CFS were compared with 36 controls on exercise measures.
2. The ME/CFS patients were split into three equal-sized groups (tertiles) by how much their cardiac output rose for each unit rise in oxygen uptake (VO2) during exercise (the Qc/VO2 slope). The authors call these "low-flow", "normal-flow" and "high-flow".
3. Skin nerve fibre density was correlated with the exercise measures.

The paper cites ethics approvals (Protocols 2018P001903 and 2019P000039), but there was no pre-registration and no published analysis plan, which is normal for a retrospective chart review. But it means that the tertile split, the choice of outcomes and the comparisons were all chosen after the data existed. The paper does not say when the tertile analysis was decided on.

The most important design feature is how patients were selected. Figure 1 shows the steps. From 1,516 tests, 917 were removed for missing data, a submaximal test, pulmonary hypertension, a lung limit to exercise, or a right atrial pressure (RAP) of 6.5 mm Hg or more. That left 599 tests with low RAP. This is the group the authors' earlier work called "preload failure": too little blood filling the heart during exercise. Of those 599, 376 had no skin biopsy. Of the 223 with a biopsy, 160 met ME/CFS criteria on record review. So every patient in the study was selected because they had (a) low filling pressure and (b) a skin biopsy.

## Sample selection

### Case definition and post-exertional malaise

ME/CFS was confirmed by reviewing medical records against the 2015 National Academy of Medicine (formerly Institute of Medicine) criteria: at least six months of fatigue, post-exertional malaise (PEM) and unrefreshing sleep, plus cognitive impairment or orthostatic intolerance. When the records were unclear, a short telephone questionnaire was used. The paper does not say:

- how PEM was judged from the records, or what the telephone questions were;
- how many patients needed the telephone questionnaire;
- whether PEM had to meet any duration or severity threshold.

No validated instrument such as the DePaul Symptom Questionnaire was used. Requiring PEM is a strength. But PEM documented in a clinic note is a weak test of it, because clinicians note worsening after exertion for many reasons.

On the other hand, these patients had a much more thorough work-up than most ME/CFS cohorts. Every one had a right heart catheterisation and a maximal invasive exercise test that ruled out heart failure, pulmonary hypertension and lung disease as the cause of exercise limitation. That removes several common mimics. Using the guide's terms, the share of true ME/CFS cases is probably higher than for a diagnosis by a general practitioner alone. But it cannot be estimated with any precision, because the PEM step is unclear. Also, this is a dyspnea-clinic population, referred because of breathlessness or exercise limitation. That is a particular slice of people with ME/CFS.

### Severity, duration and comorbidity

Severity and illness duration are not reported. Every patient was able to travel to the hospital, undergo catheterisation and cycle upright to exhaustion. That points to a mild-to-moderate population. The results say nothing about severe or very severe ME/CFS.

PEM status at the time of testing is not reported, and nothing was recorded after the test. So the study cannot say anything about PEM itself. It is about acute exercise capacity on one day.

Table 1 reports these associated conditions in the 160 patients: SFN 49 (31%), postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) 52 (33%), fibromyalgia 35 (22%), mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) "11 (22)", and a preceding infection 39 (24%). The MCAS figure does not add up: 11 of 160 is 7%, not 22%. It is a typo of some kind; which number is right cannot be told from the paper. How POTS, fibromyalgia, MCAS and preceding infection were diagnosed is not stated. Presumably it was from the records. The authors do not report results separately for patients with and without POTS, which matters because POTS has its own known high-flow and low-flow patterns (the authors cite this, reference 13).

### The selection on low filling pressure

Patients were included only if their RAP was below 6.5 mm Hg. The authors acknowledge this "may have eliminated some ME/CFS patients with peak RAP >6.5". Figure 1 shows 178 tests excluded for high RAP, and these were never checked for ME/CFS. So the study cannot tell us what share of people with ME/CFS have low filling pressures. It describes ME/CFS patients who were already known to have them.

### The selection on skin biopsy

Biopsies were taken "at the discretion of the referring physician". Only 223 of the 599 low-RAP patients (37%) had one. A physician is more likely to order a skin biopsy when they suspect neuropathy, for example because of burning pain or numbness. The authors acknowledge that this referral bias "might skew our sample to inflate prevalence of SFN". This means the 31% SFN figure is the prevalence among patients whose doctor thought a biopsy was worthwhile. It is not the prevalence in ME/CFS.

There is an unused comparison group here: the 63 low-RAP patients who had a biopsy but did not meet ME/CFS criteria. Their SFN rate would show whether SFN goes with ME/CFS or simply with low filling pressure, or with being referred for a biopsy. The paper does not report it.

### Controls

The 36 controls were patients referred to the same clinic for exertional intolerance whose iCPET was normal. "Normal" was defined as no pulmonary hypertension or heart failure, and peak oxygen uptake (VO2), cardiac output and oxygen extraction all above 80% of predicted, along with a gap of less than 10% between percent-predicted cardiac output and percent-predicted VO2. That last rule also constrains the relation between cardiac output and VO2 that the patients' tertiles are built on. The authors explain why healthy volunteers were not used, and that is reasonable for an invasive test.

But it has a large consequence. The controls were chosen *because* their exercise results were normal. So when the paper reports that patients had lower peak VO2 and lower extraction than controls, part of that difference was built in by the selection rules. The RAP difference is even more direct: patients were selected for RAP below 6.5, while controls were not. The abstract's lead result, that patients "averaged lower RAP (1.9 ± 2 vs 8.3 ± 1.5; P < .0001)", is mainly a result of the inclusion rule, not a finding. The guide flags this kind of circularity.

The controls also differ in ways that matter. They were older (56 ± 12 vs 47 ± 16 years), much more often male (44% vs 78% female), heavier, more often hypertensive (41% vs 21%) and more often on statins. Activity level and fitness are not reported for either group. Percent-predicted values adjust peak VO2 and cardiac output for age and sex, but RAP, pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP), blood pressure and extraction are not adjusted.

The controls had no skin biopsies. The authors say this was unnecessary because the laboratory has norms from more than 450 healthy people. That is a fair argument for the SFN prevalence. But Table 1 then lists SFN in the controls as "0 (0)" with P < .0001. Zero out of 36 untested people is not a measurement, and that P value has no meaning. The same may apply to MCAS and preceding infection (both "0 (0)"), since the paper does not say how they were ascertained in controls.

## Outcome measures

Most of the exercise measures here are near the objective end of the guide's scale. Filling pressures, cardiac output by the direct Fick method, arterial and mixed-venous oxygen content, and venous oxygen tension are measured from blood and catheters. A patient cannot easily change them by trying harder or less hard at a given workload. This is the paper's main strength.

Peak VO2 is more effort-dependent. The authors excluded tests with a peak heart rate below 80% of predicted or a peak respiratory exchange ratio (RER) below 1.05. These are standard but lenient effort criteria. They reduce, but do not remove, the chance that lower peak values partly reflect stopping earlier. Peak lactate was lower in the high-flow tertile (5.5 ± 2 mmol/L) than in controls (7 ± 2.2). That fits with less muscle oxygen use, but it also fits with somewhat less effort or less muscle mass recruited. The paper cannot separate these.

Skin biopsies were read by "skilled morphologists unaware of patient designations", with a standard cutoff of the 5th centile of the laboratory's age-, sex- and race-adjusted norms. That is good practice. The biopsy is a single site just above the ankle. The authors correctly note it is less sensitive for patchy, proximal or non-length-dependent SFN.

Blood volume was not measured. Low filling pressures may simply reflect low blood volume (hypovolemia), which has been reported in ME/CFS and POTS. Fluid intake, fasting before catheterisation and time of day are not reported. Fourteen patients (9%) were on diuretics. This matters for interpreting low RAP: blood pooling in the legs because of nerve damage and too little blood overall make different predictions, and the study cannot tell them apart.

## Bias

The authors' prior frameworks are visible in how the results are read. Systrom's group introduced the "preload failure" concept in earlier iCPET work (references 11 and 12). Oaklander's group has published widely on SFN in fibromyalgia and related syndromes (references 5, 10, 21, 36, 42 and others), and her laboratory read the biopsies here. Both frameworks are reasonable hypotheses. But the study was built on them, selected patients by them, and interprets results in their terms even when the data do not support that, as with the SFN findings below. This is the kind of author-preconception risk the guide asks reviewers to weigh. It is not a suggestion of misconduct.

Funding is declared: Systrom from the Solve ME/CFS Initiative and the Open Medicine Foundation, Oaklander from the National Institutes of Health and private foundations. No conflicts of interest are declared. Patient-charity funding is not in itself a bias concern.

Standard risk-of-bias problems are also present: retrospective data, selection on the outcome, unmatched symptomatic controls, no pre-specified analysis plan, and no adjustment for confounders.

## Statistical and methodological issues

**The tertile results are close to arithmetic.** The Fick principle says oxygen uptake equals cardiac output times the arterial-venous oxygen difference (VO2 = Qc × Ca-vO2). If a patient's cardiac output rises steeply for each unit rise in oxygen uptake (high Qc/VO2 slope), then each unit of blood must be giving up less oxygen. So the finding that the high-flow tertile had "impaired" extraction (0.74 vs 0.88 and 0.86) follows largely from how the tertiles were defined. Equally, the low-flow tertile having lower peak cardiac output relative to oxygen uptake follows from its low slope. The slope is a rest-to-peak change and the comparisons are of peak values, so the link is not exact. But the paper presents these as two discovered phenotypes, when they are mostly two ends of the variable used to sort patients. The authors discuss a related assumption, that the Fick variables are independent at peak exercise, but not this one.

**Tertiles always make three groups.** Splitting any continuous variable into thirds produces a "low", a "middle" and a "high" group, whether or not there are real subtypes. The paper shows no evidence of distinct clusters, such as a two-peaked distribution of the slope. The claim of "two types of peripheral neurovascular dysregulation" therefore rests on a split, not on a demonstrated grouping.

**The low-flow tertile's cardiac output was not shown to be abnormal.** Peak cardiac output in the low-flow tertile was 88.4% ± 19% of predicted, against 98.5% ± 13.3% in controls. Table 2 does not mark this as significantly different from controls. It differed only from the other two tertiles. An average of 88% is also above the 80% threshold the paper itself uses to define a normal result. The abstract nevertheless describes this as "depressed Qc from impaired venous return".

**Six patients are missing from the biopsy-by-tertile rows.** The biopsy rows of Table 2 give tertile sizes of 51, 52 and 51 (for example 18 of 51 is 35.3%), which adds up to 154, not 160. All 49 SFN cases are included, so the 6 missing patients all had non-SFN biopsies. This is not explained. No patient numbers are given for the exercise rows of Table 2 or for Figure 2, so it is unclear whether these patients are also missing there.

**Reporting errors.** Besides the MCAS percentage, the abstract gives extraction as "0.74% ± 0.1%" (it is a ratio, not a percentage). Methods say non-normal data are reported as "median ± interquartile range", while Table 2 shows medians with unlabelled values in brackets, which appear to be interquartile ranges. Neither changes a conclusion, but together they suggest the tables were not closely checked.

**Many comparisons.** Table 2 compares 20 exercise and blood-pressure variables across four groups, plus 4 biopsy rows across the three tertiles, with Tukey correction within each variable but none across variables. The correlation analyses in Figure 3 (four exercise measures against nerve fibre density) were all null, so multiplicity does not create false positives there.

**No adjustment.** Group comparisons are one-way analyses of variance with no adjustment for age, sex or hypertension, despite large imbalances. Sex distribution by tertile is not reported.

## Biological plausibility

Low filling pressures during exercise in a subset of ME/CFS patients is plausible. It fits with reports of reduced blood volume and orthostatic intolerance in ME/CFS, and with the overlap with POTS. Low systemic oxygen extraction during exercise also matches an earlier non-invasive study (Vermeulen and Vermeulen van Eck 2014, reference 35). Here the invasive data are a real step up: extraction was directly measured from mixed-venous blood, not estimated.

The specific mechanism proposed for the high-flow group, blood bypassing capillaries through nerve-damaged arteriovenous shunts, is speculative. The evidence offered is from skin of the palm in fibromyalgia and from skin in complex regional pain syndrome (references 14 and 34), not from exercising muscle. Low extraction with high flow has other explanations. The authors name one, mitochondrial myopathy (MM), whose "hallmark during incremental exercise is an increased Qc/VO2 slope". That is exactly the high-flow tertile's defining feature. MM was not tested for: there were no muscle biopsies, no genetics and no near-infrared spectroscopy. Yet the paper says "these exclusions leave systemic microcirculatory dysfunction... as the presumptive mechanism". MM was not excluded; only intracardiac shunting was. Other possibilities include blood flow to non-exercising tissue and lower muscle mass recruited. The study cannot tell any of these apart.

The SFN mechanism also faces the guide's "natural experiment" test. SFN is common, for example in diabetes, and autonomic neuropathies that cause venous pooling exist on their own. Those conditions are not generally known for PEM. The paper does not discuss this.

The study does not address PEM at all: not the delay in onset, not the duration, not the fact that cognitive and emotional exertion can trigger it. The authors frame their goal as "acute exertional intolerance", which is honest. But the Interpretation says peripheral vascular dysregulation may cause "ME/CFS's major symptom—exertional intolerance". In ME/CFS the defining feature of exertional intolerance is the delayed worsening, which this study did not measure.

## Interpretation and spin

**On SFN.** The data show that SFN was equally common in all three tertiles (35.3%, 28.8%, 31.4%; P = .61). Nerve fibre density did not correlate with peak VO2, peak cardiac output, extraction or peak RAP (Figure 3, all r between −0.13 and −0.005). The abstract states this plainly: "Denervation severity did not correlate with exertional measures." That is a null result for the SFN hypothesis, within the limits of a single distal biopsy.

The discussion reads it the other way. It says "Our iCPET data associate SFN with both low-flow and high-flow ME/CFS profiles" and concludes "we propose SFN as the major cause of preload failure in a substantial, not yet fully measured, proportion of ME/CFS patients". The abstract says "In patients with small-fiber pathology, neuropathic dysregulation causing microvascular dilation may limit exertion". Equal SFN rates in every tertile is not an association with each profile. It is an absence of association with any of them. The authors suggest the single-site biopsy may explain the null correlation, which is fair, but a measure that cannot detect the effect cannot also support the claim.

The paper also says "We also report the first association of small-fiber pathology with ME/CFS". Without a comparison group that had biopsies, and with biopsy referral bias the authors acknowledge, the study shows a high rate of abnormal biopsies in a selected group. That is a prevalence, not an association. It is a meaningful prevalence: under the laboratory's norms about 5% of healthy people fall at or below the 5th centile, and the median centile was 21 to 26 in each tertile rather than about 50. But how much referral explains is unknown.

**On shunting.** The abstract's results section says impaired extraction was found "in association with peripheral left-to-right shunting". Peripheral shunting was not measured. It was inferred by excluding intracardiac shunting, while not excluding other causes. Stating it as a result goes beyond the data.

**On deconditioning.** The paper says: "The current study definitively eliminates this possibility". It gives two reasons. First, the hallmark of deconditioning is low peak cardiac output, while the study found increased cardiac output. Second, detrained hearts show higher filling pressures, while these patients had abnormally low ones. The first reason applies only to the high-flow tertile. The low-flow tertile had lower peak cardiac output, which by the paper's own criterion is what deconditioning would look like. The second reason applies to all patients, but every patient was selected for low filling pressure. So it shows only that these selected patients lack the high-pressure pattern, and it rests on cited literature rather than on any comparison in this study. The study measured no physical activity, had no deconditioned comparison group, and its controls were older and possibly no fitter. The cited literature makes deconditioning a less likely sole explanation, especially for the high-flow pattern. But "definitively eliminates" is not supported.

**On treatment.** The limitations section says "We address this in the clinic by encouraging fitness with a careful, graded exercise program". This is a description of clinic practice, not a finding of the study. It sits oddly next to the paper's own requirement that every patient has PEM. The study provides no evidence about exercise programmes either way.

**What the paper gets right.** The Interpretation section says the findings "generate the hypothesis" that peripheral vascular dysregulation causes exertional intolerance, and that "more comprehensive evaluation is recommended". That framing is appropriate. The problem is that the abstract and discussion state much more than this.

## What the study demonstrates

It shows that, in a referral population of ME/CFS patients selected for low filling pressures and for having a skin biopsy:

- peak oxygen uptake averaged about 80% of predicted, with low right atrial and wedge pressures during upright exercise, and no heart or lung disease to explain it;
- the cardiac output response to exercise varied widely, and the patients with the steepest rise had low oxygen extraction and high oxygen in mixed-venous blood at peak exercise;
- about a third of the biopsies met the standard cutoff for SFN, and the whole distribution of nerve fibre density was shifted down compared with laboratory norms;
- nerve fibre density was not related to any of the four exercise measures it was tested against (peak VO2, peak cardiac output, extraction and peak RAP), and SFN was equally common in each flow group.

These are real, carefully measured physiological data in a population where invasive data are rare.

It does not show:

- that low filling pressure is common in ME/CFS (patients were selected for it);
- that ME/CFS patients differ from controls on RAP, VO2 or extraction in a way that was not created by the selection rules;
- that there are two distinct physiological subtypes;
- that peripheral shunting occurs, or that mitochondrial problems were excluded;
- that SFN is more common in ME/CFS than in other referred patients, or that SFN causes any of the exercise findings;
- that deconditioning has been ruled out;
- anything about PEM.

## Critical missing elements

- SFN rate in the 63 non-ME/CFS low-RAP patients who had biopsies.
- How PEM was ascertained, and the telephone questionnaire.
- ME/CFS status of the 178 patients excluded for high RAP, and of controls.
- Results separated by POTS status.
- Blood volume, or at least fluid and fasting status before testing.
- Physical activity or any deconditioning measure.
- Illness duration and severity.
- Sex distribution and age by tertile; adjustment for age and sex.
- The six patients missing from the biopsy-by-tertile rows.
- Any test for distinct clusters rather than tertiles.
- Any direct test of the high-flow mechanism: muscle oxygenation, muscle biopsy, or a measure of peripheral shunting.

## Recommendations

**For patients.** This study supports the idea that some people with ME/CFS have measurable problems getting blood back to the heart, or getting oxygen into muscles, during exercise. It does not show that nerve damage causes these, and it does not test any treatment. Its comment about graded exercise in clinic is not based on its data.

**For clinicians.** Invasive testing can find objective abnormalities in some patients with ME/CFS who have unexplained exercise limitation. A skin biopsy showing SFN is a finding in its own right, but this study gives no reason to think it explains the patient's exercise intolerance.

**For researchers.** Repeat the physiology in an unselected, consecutive ME/CFS cohort, with PEM measured by a validated questionnaire, against sedentary controls matched for age and sex. Measure blood volume. Test the high-flow group directly for mitochondrial and microvascular causes. Report SFN against a biopsied comparison group. Pre-register the subgrouping method, and test for clusters rather than imposing tertiles.

## Final assessment

The paper's strength is its measurements. Invasive exercise testing gives some of the most objective data on exercise physiology that exist, and the effort criteria, blinded biopsy reading and exclusion of heart and lung disease are all good practice. The descriptive finding, that a subset of ME/CFS patients have low filling pressures and some have low oxygen extraction with high flow, is credible.

But the study's main claims are much stronger than its design allows. Patients were selected for low filling pressure, and controls for normal results, so the headline group differences are largely produced by the selection rules. The two "phenotypes" come from splitting a continuous variable into thirds, and their defining differences follow largely from the Fick equation. The SFN hypothesis was effectively tested and not supported: SFN was equally common in every group and correlated with none of the four exercise measures tested. Yet the discussion proposes SFN as "the major cause of preload failure". Peripheral shunting is stated as a result without being measured, mitochondrial causes are said to be excluded when they were not tested, and deconditioning is said to be "definitively" eliminated without data on it.

What prevents a higher score: selection on the outcome for both patients and controls; the circular RAP comparison leading the abstract; tertiles presented as subtypes; conclusions about SFN that run against the paper's own null results; and no information on PEM ascertainment, severity, duration or blood volume.

What prevents a lower score: the measurements themselves are objective and well done; the data are reported in enough detail to see the problems; the authors acknowledge several limitations, including referral bias and the RAP cut-off; the Interpretation is framed as hypothesis-generating; and the descriptive physiology in a carefully worked-up group has real value as a starting point.

## Executive summary

This study looked back at the records of 160 people with ME/CFS who had an invasive exercise test, where catheters measure the heart's pumping and blood oxygen while the person cycles. All had also had a skin biopsy to look for damage to small nerves. It compared them with 36 patients from the same clinic whose test came back normal.

The measurements are among the most objective available, and the study gives rare data. Some patients had low filling of the heart during exercise. Some had blood returning from the body still unusually full of oxygen, meaning muscles took less of it out. About a third of skin biopsies showed small nerve fibre damage.

But the patients were chosen because they had low heart filling, and the controls because their results were normal. So the main differences between the groups were largely built in by the way people were picked. The two "types" of patient come from splitting one measure into thirds, and their differences follow largely from the arithmetic of that measure. Nerve damage was equally common in every group and was unrelated to the four exercise results it was tested against. Still, the paper proposes nerve damage as the major cause. It also states that blood bypasses the muscles without having measured it, and says deconditioning is "definitively" ruled out without data on fitness. The study did not measure post-exertional malaise.

It is a useful starting point for hypotheses about blood flow in ME/CFS, not evidence for the mechanisms it proposes.

Grade: 4.5 / 10.
