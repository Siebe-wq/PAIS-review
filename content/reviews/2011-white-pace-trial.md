## Executive summary

PACE (short for "pacing, graded activity, and cognitive behaviour therapy: a randomised evaluation") was a large UK trial in 641 people with chronic fatigue syndrome. It compared four treatments over a year. All four groups got specialist medical care (SMC). Three groups also got one extra therapy: adaptive pacing therapy (APT), cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) or graded exercise therapy (GET).

The paper concludes that CBT and GET "can safely be added to SMC to moderately improve outcomes", and that APT does not help. That conclusion rests on two questionnaires the patients filled in themselves. The patients knew which treatment they had. Two of the therapies told them their symptoms were not a sign of harm and that they could do more. The questionnaires then asked how fatigued and limited they felt.

That is the core problem. In an unblinded trial, self-rated outcomes are open to expectation and reporting bias. In this trial the risk is higher than usual, because CBT works directly on how patients interpret and report their symptoms. The objective measures that could have checked the self-reports mostly did not move. Fitness did not improve with GET. Days lost from work and receipt of benefits did not differ between groups. A walking test gave a small gain for GET only, with a third of that group missing. The main outcomes were also changed after data collection had finished. Under the original protocol's definitions, improvement and recovery rates were low and the differences were much weaker.

The trial is large, randomised and well followed up. It is the most influential trial of these treatments. But its design cannot separate a real change in health from a change in how people rated their health. Grade: **3 / 10**.

## Study design

A parallel-group randomised trial with four arms, run at six specialist chronic fatigue clinics in the UK National Health Service. Recruitment ran from March 2005 to November 2008. Outcomes were measured at 12, 24 and 52 weeks.

Each therapy arm offered up to 14 sessions over 23 weeks, plus a booster at 36 weeks. SMC alone was at least three doctor visits. The paper describes the theories behind each therapy plainly:

- APT was based on the "envelope theory", which "regards chronic fatigue syndrome as an organic disease process that is not reversible by changes in behaviour". Participants were told not to use more than 70% of their perceived energy.
- CBT was based on "the fear avoidance theory", which regards the illness "as being reversible". It aimed "to change the behavioural and cognitive factors assumed to be responsible for perpetuation of the participant's symptoms and disability", including "fears about symptoms".
- GET was based on "deconditioning and exercise intolerance theories". It aimed to "reverse the deconditioning, and thereby reduce fatigue and disability".

**Pre-registration.** The trial was registered (ISRCTN54285094), and its protocol was published in 2007 in the journal BMC Neurology. This is a real strength. It is also what lets a reader check the changes described below.

**Blinding.** None for participants, therapists or doctors. The research assessors were not masked either. Only the statistician for the primary analysis was masked. The paper says so openly. Blinding was not assessed, which is unsurprising when nobody was blinded.

**Comparison arms.** SMC alone is not a placebo. Its participants had lower expectations (41% confident it would help, against 70–72% for APT and GET) and lower satisfaction (50%, against 82–88%). APT is the better comparison for CBT and GET. It had the same number of therapy sessions, from experienced therapists, with similar therapeutic alliance. That design choice is a strength. But APT gave the opposite message: the illness is organic and symptoms are a signal to stop. So the APT comparison controls for attention, not for what patients are told to believe about their symptoms.

## Sample selection

**Case definition.** Participants met the Oxford criteria. These require only that fatigue is the main symptom, with significant disability, for six months, without an excluding diagnosis. They do not require post-exertional malaise (PEM), the worsening of symptoms after effort that defines ME/CFS in current criteria. The guide treats even the broader-than-necessary Fukuda (1994 CDC) criteria as unacceptable. The Oxford criteria are broader still.

The paper partly addresses this. Participants were also assessed against the international (CDC) criteria (427, 67%) and the London criteria for myalgic encephalomyelitis (329, 51%). The London criteria require "postexertional fatigue". The paper reports "equivalent results" in both subgroups. This helps, but it is not enough to settle the question:

- Neither definition was operationalised with a validated PEM instrument such as the DePaul Symptom Questionnaire.
- The international criteria do not require PEM. It is one of eight optional symptoms.
- The subgroup analyses carry the same blinding problem as the main analysis.

At baseline, 84% reported PEM as a symptom, on a single yes/no item. How long it lasted, how long it took to appear and what triggered it were not recorded.

**Severity.** Entry required a physical function score of 65 or less out of 100, originally 60, raised 11 months in "to increase recruitment". The mean at baseline was about 38. People "unable to attend hospital appointments" were excluded. So the housebound and bedbound were not studied, and the paper's safety claim cannot apply to them. Median illness duration was 32 months.

**Psychiatric status.** Assessed with a structured clinical interview. 47% had a mood or anxiety disorder, and 33% a depressive disorder. This was handled as a stratification factor.

**Comorbidities.** Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, orthostatic intolerance and mast cell activation syndrome were not assessed.

**Screening.** Of 3158 patients screened, 641 were recruited. The commonest reason for exclusion was not meeting Oxford criteria (1011 patients). According to the paper, 745 of these did not have chronic fatigue syndrome.

## Outcome measures

The two primary outcomes were both self-rated questionnaires:

| Outcome | Where it sits on the bias scale |
|---|---|
| Chalder fatigue questionnaire | Self-rated; high risk in an unblinded trial, and the guide notes fatigue scales are often of limited validity |
| Short form-36 (SF-36) physical function subscale | Self-rated; high risk in an unblinded trial |

Secondary outcomes reported in this paper include the participant-rated clinical global impression of change, the work and social adjustment scale, sleep, the hospital anxiety and depression scale, and symptom counts. All are self-rated.

The one objective measure reported is the **6-minute walking test**. The guide places it at high risk of performance bias, because people walk further when they expect to be able to. GET walked 35 m further than SMC at 52 weeks (p=0.0002). That is about 10% over a year in a group whose therapy was mostly walking. CBT did not differ from SMC. Only 69% of the GET group and 74% of the SMC group did the test at 52 weeks, so the missing data are large and uneven.

The protocol listed more objective measures. They were not reported in this paper:

- **A step test of fitness.** Results appeared four years later in a separate mediation paper. They showed no difference between groups, including GET.
- **Employment and benefits**, measured with the Client Service Receipt Inventory. These were reported in the 2012 cost-effectiveness paper. In the year after randomisation, 84–89% of each group lost time from work, with similar mean days lost; benefit receipt also did not differ.
- **Actigraphy**, a wrist-worn movement monitor. This was worn at baseline only. The published 2007 protocol already listed it only as a baseline predictor, not as an outcome.

So every measure that could not be shaped by how a patient chose to answer was either null or modest and confined to GET.

## Bias

**Performance and reporting bias.** This is the decisive issue. The paper accepts that "primary outcomes were subjective and rated by participants" and that this "could be subject to other biases". Its defence is that CBT did well despite lower expectations (57% confident) than APT (72%). That is a fair point, and it should be weighed. A pure expectation story would predict APT doing better than it did.

But expectation at the start is not the only path. CBT's stated aim is to change how participants interpret their symptoms. GET tells them symptoms during activity are not a sign of harm. After a year of that, asking "how fatigued are you?" partly measures whether the therapy worked on the participant's interpretation, not only on their illness. This is a response shift that a baseline expectation question cannot detect. The same applies to the PEM item, discussed under spin.

**Outcome switching.** The protocol defined the primary outcome as the proportion of "overall improvers": a 50% reduction in fatigue score or a score of 3 or less (bimodal scoring, 0–11), and an SF-36 score of 75 or more or a 50% increase. The paper replaced this with mean differences on continuous scores, and rescored the fatigue scale from bimodal to Likert (0–33). It says the changes were made "before outcome data were examined" and approved by the steering committees.

There are three reasons for concern despite that statement:

- The analysis plan was approved in May 2010, after outcome data collection finished in January 2010. The trial was unblinded, so the team saw participants and therapists throughout.
- The sample size calculation was built on the original response rates. It assumed 60% response to CBT, 50% to GET and 10% to SMC.
- The protocol's own reasoning for the original thresholds (a score of 3 or less is "normal fatigue") was dropped.

When the released data were reanalysed using the protocol's definition, improvement was 20% for CBT, 21% for GET and 10% for SMC (see context). The assumed response rates had been 60%, 50% and 10%.

**The "normal range".** A post-hoc analysis reported that 30% (CBT) and 28% (GET) were "within normal ranges" at 52 weeks. "Normal" physical function was set at 60 or more. Entry required 65 or less. A participant could therefore be "within the normal range" and ill enough to enter the trial on the same score. The fatigue threshold (18 or less, Likert) also overlaps with the entry threshold (6 or more, bimodal). The threshold came from the population mean minus one standard deviation, 84 − 24. SF-36 physical function is heavily skewed in the general population, with most people near 100. On such a distribution, the mean minus one standard deviation gives a low cut-off. The protocol had set 70 as one standard deviation below the population mean, and 85 or more for recovery.

**Author preconceptions.** The principal investigators developed and had long promoted CBT and GET for this illness. The CBT and GET manuals were "based on manuals used in previous trials", including trials by the same group. APT was created for this trial, by the same team, with input from Action for ME and Westcare. The guide counts the authors' preconceptions as the largest source of bias. Here they are not hidden. The paper's hypotheses are openly the authors' model of the illness.

**Funding and interests.** Funded by the Medical Research Council, the Department of Health for England, the Scottish Chief Scientist Office and the Department for Work and Pensions. The last of these has an interest in return to work. Four authors, including all three principal investigators, declare paid work for insurance or reinsurance companies. These are declared in the paper. Funding does not show the results are wrong. It is a reason to want the objective outcomes, which did not support the conclusions.

**In-trial messaging.** During the trial, a newsletter told participants that the national guideline recommended CBT, GET and activity management (see context). The investigators say all arms got it. In an unblinded trial with self-rated outcomes, it adds to the risk.

## Statistical and methodological issues

The modelling is competent. It uses mixed models with baseline adjustment, accounts for clustering by therapist (small intraclass correlations), applies Bonferroni correction, and runs a per-protocol sensitivity analysis. Only 10 of 641 had no outcome data. Losses to follow-up (5%) were low and even across groups.

Specific issues:

- **The "clinically useful difference" is small and post hoc.** It was defined as half the baseline standard deviation: 2 points on fatigue and 8 on physical function. The entry criteria restricted the baseline range, which shrinks the standard deviation and so the threshold. Even so, CBT against SMC on physical function (7.1 points, 95% confidence interval 2.0 to 12.1) does not reach 8. The paper says differences "almost always exceeded" the threshold.
- **The responder analysis is post hoc and generous.** "Improved" meant at least 2 points better on fatigue and 8 on physical function. By this definition, 45% of the SMC-alone group improved, against 59% (CBT) and 61% (GET).
- **Absolute levels.** At 52 weeks, mean physical function was 58 (CBT), 58 (GET) and 51 (SMC). That is still well below the population mean of 84 the authors cite.
- **Stratification errors.** Some participants were stratified wrongly at randomisation. The authors used true status in the models, which is the right fix.
- **Noise.** There are no test–retest data for these instruments in this population, and the smallest detectable change is not reported.

## Biological plausibility

GET was justified by deconditioning: fitness lost through inactivity. If GET worked by that route, fitness should rise. It did not. The step test showed no difference between groups. The paper does not report this, since the result came later, but it undercuts the mechanism the GET arm was built on.

CBT was justified by fear-avoidance: patients avoid activity out of fear, and that keeps them ill. If CBT worked that way, activity and work should increase. Work days lost did not change. Activity was not measured objectively at follow-up.

So for both therapies, the changes in self-report came without the downstream changes the authors' own models predict. The simplest explanation that fits all the data is a change in reporting, not in function. This cannot be proven from the trial, but the alternative needs more assumptions.

## Interpretation and spin

The abstract: "CBT and GET can safely be added to SMC to moderately improve outcomes for chronic fatigue syndrome." It does not say the outcomes were self-rated in an unblinded trial, or that the primary outcome had been changed.

On PEM: "The comparatively greater reduction in postexertional malaise with both CBT and GET compared with the other two treatments is notable, since the risk of exacerbation of this symptom is commonly given as a reason to avoid treatments such as GET." This was a single self-reported yes/no item. The therapies taught participants to reinterpret symptoms after activity. Fewer "yes" answers is not evidence that PEM was less frequent or less severe.

On safety: "all four treatments tested are safe", and "patients need not be concerned about safety". Serious adverse events were more common with GET than SMC (17 events in 13 participants against 7 in 7; p=0.0433). The paper says this "is unlikely to be important because serious adverse events were not thought by the independent scrutinisers to be related to treatment". The scrutinisers were unmasked when deciding that. "Serious deterioration" needed a 20-point fall on two consecutive assessments, a fairly high bar. The people most likely to be harmed, the severely affected, were excluded. And the case definition did not require PEM, which is the feature that makes exertion risky. The data support "no large excess of recorded serious harm in ambulant Oxford-criteria outpatients". They do not support a general claim of safety for ME/CFS.

On generalisability: "The PACE findings can be generalised to patients who also meet alternative diagnostic criteria for chronic fatigue syndrome and myalgic encephalomyelitis but only if fatigue is their main symptom." The qualifier matters, and it is to the paper's credit. But the subgroup results share all the bias problems above.

On mechanism, the paper makes one fair statement worth noting: "The effectiveness of behavioural treatments does not imply that the condition is psychological in nature."

The final recommendation: "Patients attending secondary care with chronic fatigue syndrome should be offered individual CBT or GET, alongside SMC." This goes beyond the evidence for the reasons above.

## What the study actually demonstrates

- In ambulant UK outpatients diagnosed by Oxford criteria, people who received CBT or GET rated their fatigue and physical function somewhat better at one year than those who did not. They knew their treatment, and the therapies targeted how they rated their symptoms.
- APT, as designed for this trial, did not improve self-ratings over SMC alone, despite high expectations. This is a real finding about this version of APT. It does not test pacing as patients practise it, and it does not test whether pacing reduces PEM.
- GET participants walked about 35 m further than SMC participants in a 6-minute test, with large and uneven missing data.
- Fitness, work and benefits did not differ (from sister papers).
- There was no large excess of recorded serious harm in this ambulant group.

It does not show:

- That CBT or GET improve the underlying illness, function or capacity.
- That they are safe for people with ME/CFS defined by PEM, or for the severely affected.
- That benefit lasts. At about 2.5 years, the groups did not differ, although crossover makes that result hard to read either way.
- Any meaningful rate of recovery.

## Critical missing elements

- An objective primary outcome, or at least objective co-primary outcomes reported in the main paper: actigraphy at follow-up, step test, employment.
- A comparison arm that controlled for what participants were told about their symptoms, not only for therapist time.
- A case definition requiring PEM, with PEM measured by a validated instrument.
- Primary outcomes as specified in the protocol, reported alongside the revised ones.
- Inclusion of housebound patients, or a clear statement that safety claims do not apply to them.
- Patient-level harm data with masked judgement of whether harms were treatment-related.
- Release of the data. It came only after a tribunal order (see context).

## Recommendations

**For patients and clinicians.** This trial does not show that CBT or GET improve ME/CFS. It shows that people who received them reported feeling somewhat better on questionnaires, without matching changes in fitness or work. It does not show GET is safe for people whose defining symptom is PEM.

**For guideline writers.** Treat PACE's efficacy outcomes as high risk of bias. Weight the objective outcomes and the protocol-defined analyses. The 2021 guideline from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) did this.

**For trialists.** Pair any unblinded behavioural intervention with objective outcomes as primary, measured with the same rigour. Activity monitoring, return to work and validated PEM measures are the obvious candidates. Report the protocol-defined outcomes even if the analysis plan changes them.

## Final assessment

**Grade: 3 / 10.**

What prevents a higher score:

- An unblinded trial with self-rated primary outcomes, where two therapies directly targeted how participants interpret and report symptoms. That alone means the design cannot separate a treatment effect from reporting bias. Under the guide, the worst domain sets the ceiling, and this domain is fatal for the efficacy claim.
- The primary outcomes and thresholds were changed after data collection ended. The protocol-defined results are much weaker.
- The "normal range" overlapped the entry criteria.
- The objective outcomes were null (fitness, work, benefits) or modest, GET-only and heavily missing (walking). The null ones were not in the main paper.
- The case definition did not require PEM.
- The safety claim goes beyond a sample that excluded the severely affected.

What prevents a lower score:

- The trial is large, randomised and pre-registered with a published protocol.
- Loss to follow-up was low and analysis methods were sound.
- It used an attention-matched comparison (APT) and reported unflattering details openly: expectations by arm, SMC's extra sessions, the change of entry threshold.
- The numbers it reports are internally consistent. The problem is what self-ratings in this design can carry, not fabricated or contradictory data. Its own walking test result is modestly in its favour for GET.
- The paper names its main limitation ("Primary outcomes were subjective and rated by participants") even while understating its consequences.

Confidence is high. I read the full paper, the 2007 protocol and the 2018 reanalysis. I did not read the web appendix, which holds forest plots, the per-protocol tables and the serious adverse event listings. I checked the work data in the 2012 cost-effectiveness paper directly. The step test and benefits results come from the reanalysis's account of the sister papers; I did not read those papers myself.
