## Executive summary

This observational study of 103 patients examined subcutaneous lidocaine-HP-β-CD for treating
post-COVID symptoms. The intervention is biologically plausible and the study documents
improvement in quality-of-life metrics. But the methodology has flaws that prevent any
meaningful conclusion about treatment efficacy. The lack of a control group, the high risk of
bias from unblinded treatment, the absence of objective outcome measures, and significant
commercial conflicts of interest together undermine the study's validity.

## Study design

**Design type — critical flaw.** An interrupted time series with patients as their own
controls. There is no control group whatsoever. That makes it impossible to separate a
treatment effect from natural recovery, regression to the mean, placebo effect or temporal
trends. This is particularly problematic for a condition with a known variable trajectory and
spontaneous improvement. The average illness duration of 2.5 years means some patients may have
been entering a natural recovery phase.

**Blinding — critical flaw.** Completely unblinded. Patients knew they were receiving
treatment, which creates large potential for placebo and expectation effects, response bias on
subjective measures, and performance bias. There was no masking of outcome assessors.

## Sample selection

**Post-COVID diagnostic criteria — major concern.** Duration of at least 12 weeks post-infection
is adequate. But COVID confirmation is not specified in the available materials, and
hospitalisation status is not clearly specified. An average duration of 2.5 years suggests a
moderate-to-severe cohort.

**Symptom characterisation — moderate concern.** The study mentions PEM and other ME/CFS-like
symptoms, but there is no clear statement about PEM assessment methodology and no use of
validated PEM questionnaires such as the DSQ-2. The proportion with ME/CFS overlap is unknown,
and there is no characterisation of POTS/OI or MCAS comorbidities.

**Sample characteristics.** n=103, adequate for an observational study but insufficient without
controls. Mean age 48. Mean duration 2.5 years, so a very chronic cohort. The claim that
patients had "no improvement" prior to treatment is impossible to verify.

## Outcome measures — critical flaw

**SF-12 Physical Component Score.** Small effect at 24 weeks, improving from about 30 to about
33 points. This is almost entirely subjective, and so highly susceptible to expectation and
placebo effects. Performance bias applies too: patients may try harder at activities because
they expect to do better.

**SF-12 Mental Component Score.** Moderate effect at 24 weeks, improving from about 32 to about
38-39 points. Even more subjective than the physical measure. Mental health improvements can
follow from hope, attention and belief in a treatment.

**Symptom burden.** Improvements across multiple domains — PEM, dyspnea, fatigue, cognitive
problems, pain. All patient-reported, all subjective, none validated through objective measures,
and all at high risk of reporting bias in an unblinded study.

**What is missing.** No blood biomarkers, no cardiopulmonary exercise testing, no tilt table
testing for POTS/OI, no imaging, no actigraphy, no lean test — no physiological measure that
cannot be influenced by effort or perception. This is hard to excuse, for three reasons. These
patients were monitored with apps and measurement tools, yet no objective physiological data is
reported. The clinic claims lidocaine has anti-inflammatory effects, but there are no
inflammation markers. There are claims about autonomic dysfunction, but no autonomic testing.

## Bias — extreme

**Financial conflicts of interest, severe.** The study was funded by Excellent Care Clinics, the
private clinic providing the treatment. The lead authors are physicians at that clinic who
developed and administer the treatment. The clinic's business model depends on the treatment
being seen as effective, and it openly discusses plans for commercialisation and insurance
reimbursement. This is a textbook case of research funded by those with a direct financial stake
in positive results.

**Investigator bias, severe.** The researchers developed the treatment protocol themselves, have
reputational investment in its success, and are actively promoting it to patients and insurers.
No independent oversight is apparent.

**Publication bias risk.** Were there pilot studies with negative results that were not
published? Was the successful cohort selected for publication?

**Design choices that amplify bias.** No control group, which lets natural history and placebo
appear as treatment effect. No blinding, which maximises placebo and expectation effects.
Reliance solely on subjective outcomes, which maximises the impact of bias. A 4-week
pre-treatment baseline, which creates a regression-to-the-mean opportunity. Patients as their own
controls, which is vulnerable to temporal confounding.

## Statistical and methodological issues

**Regression to the mean — major problem.** Patients were selected on severe, longstanding
symptoms. Natural fluctuation in chronic conditions means they are likely to improve somewhat
regardless. Without controls this cannot be separated from a treatment effect.

**Temporal confounding.** Treatment started at a specific calendar time, so any concurrent
factors — seasonal changes, pandemic phase changes, other treatments, life changes — are
confounded with treatment. The 36-week follow-up spans multiple seasons.

**Selection bias.** Who self-selects to travel to a private Dutch clinic for experimental
treatment? Likely a higher-SES, more health-literate, more motivated group, which may have a
different trajectory from the general Long Covid population. There is no data on patients who
were screened but not included.

**Attrition.** The extension to 36 weeks covered only the first group, and it is not explained
why. How many dropped out? Were dropouts due to adverse events or lack of efficacy?

## Biological plausibility — moderate to good

Lidocaine has documented anti-inflammatory properties, immune dysregulation is implicated in
post-COVID, targeting lymph nodes for immune modulation is conceptually reasonable, and there is
some pre-clinical evidence for lidocaine effects on NETs and cytokines.

But: is subcutaneous administration actually achieving therapeutic levels systemically? No
pharmacokinetic data is provided. There is no measurement of inflammation markers to show target
engagement. Previous studies used IV lidocaine, and a different route may have different
effects. The HP-β-CD formulation is novel, with no data on whether it actually improves delivery.

On dose and safety, home self-administration raises adherence and monitoring questions, and
"the majority reported non-serious skin problems at the injection site" is not quantified.

## Interpretation and spin — moderate concern

What they claim: "78% reported clear and clinically relevant improvement"; "significantly
improved quality of life and symptom burden"; "offers a scalable and potentially
disease-modifying approach".

What the data shows: subjective improvements in unblinded patients without controls;
small-to-moderate effect sizes on subjective scales; no evidence of disease modification, since
there are no objective measures of pathology; no evidence of mechanism, since there are no
biomarkers.

"Disease-modifying" is an extraordinary claim with no supporting evidence here. Media coverage
emphasised "80% improvement" without context about the study's limitations, and the clinic is
already marketing the treatment while calling for further research.

## What this study actually demonstrates

It shows that patients receiving this treatment reported feeling better, that the treatment
appears tolerable (though adverse event data is incomplete), and that there is enough of a signal
to warrant properly controlled investigation.

It does not demonstrate treatment efficacy, does not rule out placebo effect, does not
demonstrate a mechanism of action, and does not establish that the treatment beats natural
history.

## Critical missing elements

1. A control group, placebo or usual care
2. Blinding, at minimum of outcome assessors
3. Objective outcome measures
4. Biomarker data on inflammation and immune function
5. Detailed safety data with full adverse event characterisation
6. Pharmacokinetic data on lidocaine levels actually achieved
7. Target engagement data, as proof of mechanism
8. Independent funding and investigators
9. A pre-registered analysis plan
10. Clear diagnostic criteria, especially for the PEM/ME/CFS phenotype

## Comparison to field standards

This study would not meet quality standards for regulatory approval, practice guideline
inclusion, Cochrane systematic review inclusion (too high a risk of bias), or insurance coverage
decisions in most healthcare systems.

For reference, the PACE trial — rightly criticised for methodological flaws — at least had
control groups, blinding of outcome assessors though not participants, and some objective
measures in the form of actigraphy. This study has none of those, and adds severe financial
conflicts of interest.

## Recommendations

**For patients.** These results cannot be used to judge whether the treatment works. The
improvements may reflect placebo, natural recovery or other factors. Paying privately for an
unproven treatment should be considered carefully. Wait for properly controlled trials.

**For researchers.** Run a randomised placebo-controlled trial with adequate blinding (saline
injections as placebo), pre-registered primary outcomes, a mix of subjective and objective
measures, independent funding, and longer follow-up to assess durability. Add mechanistic
studies: measure inflammatory markers before, during and after treatment; do PK/PD analysis;
identify responder characteristics. And phenotype better: separate PEM-predominant from other
phenotypes, assess POTS/OI and MCAS systematically, and check whether mechanism differs by
subgroup.

**For clinicians.** This is preliminary, hypothesis-generating data only. The treatment cannot
be recommended on this evidence. Explain the limitations to patients who are considering it.

## Final assessment

**Grade: 3.5 out of 10.**

Why it does not pass: the absence of a control group is fatal for causal inference; there is no
blinding on highly subjective outcomes; the financial conflicts of interest are severe; there
are no objective outcome measures; and the risk of bias is too high to draw meaningful
conclusions.

What prevents a lower score: the sample size is adequate for this design, the biological
mechanism is plausible, effect sizes appear consistent across outcomes, follow-up duration is
reasonable, and symptom tracking is detailed.

Why not higher: it would need a control group, blinding, objective measures and independent
funding to score above 5. The current design cannot distinguish efficacy from bias, placebo or
natural history.

## Conclusion

This study is the kind of preliminary, commercially motivated research that has dogged the
ME/CFS and Long Covid fields. The intervention deserves proper investigation, but this paper
cannot support conclusions about efficacy. No control group, no blinding, only subjective
outcomes and severe conflicts of interest together produce an evidence base that is close to
worthless for clinical decision-making.

The biological rationale is reasonable enough to justify a proper RCT, but patients and insurers
should not be making treatment decisions on this data. The enthusiastic media coverage and
clinical promotion ahead of adequate evidence is concerning, and potentially harmful to the
field's credibility.

The most charitable reading is that this is very preliminary data suggesting a signal worth
investigating properly. The concerning reading is that it is commercially motivated research
designed to generate marketing material for a private clinic's profit-making treatment, with
methodology inadequate to determine efficacy. The truth is probably in between, but the evidence
quality is far too low to make any claim about treatment efficacy.
